Impact Labs was a platform for helping donors make smarter philanthropic
decisions. I started it over the pandemic with my friend Sreekar, and we worked
on it from 2022 to 2025.

The idea, in one line: an index fund for philanthropy. We worked closely with
nonprofits to gather real data on their impact, translated that impact into a
score, and organized the highest-performing programs into cause-based *funds*
that donors could subscribe to. A fund was a curated basket of programs
attacking one cause — climate, refugee relief, homelessness — from multiple
angles. You subscribed, and each month you got a report on exactly where your
money went and who it helped. As the real-world context of a cause shifted, we
rebalanced the basket, so you were always funding the most effective mix.

Three convictions shaped everything we built.

## Nuance over a single number

Everyone wants a star rating. We refused to give one.

We scored each program from 1 to 5 on four separate axes — the **depth** of its
impact, the **breadth** of it, the **evidence** behind it, and the **timeline**
to see it. Those scores came from a weighted model built out of academic papers,
industry analyst review, and interviews with nonprofit leaders in the space. We'd
define an impact model that identified all of a program's *outputs*, then weight
those outputs by what the research said actually mattered.

We never collapsed the four axes into one headline number, and that was the whole
point. Donors already care about a cause — what they lacked was a way to have a
more nuanced conversation about it. What *kind* of impact? Over what horizon?
Backed by how much evidence? For whom, specifically? Roll those four axes up
across a whole fund and you get its *shape* — the average of every program in the
basket, still never crushed into one headline number. Switch funds to see how the
shape changes:

## Outcomes, not overhead

The second conviction was a stake in the ground about how you're allowed to judge
a nonprofit.

People love to force a poverty mindset onto the sector: that a good nonprofit
should run on fumes, that spending on salaries or equipment or leadership is a
betrayal of the mission. That's not realistic, and it's not how impact works.
Organizations need good talent and good tools to do good work. So we ignored
overhead entirely. We parsed 990 forms, impact reports, and worked directly with
organizations to understand their outputs — then mapped their *total revenue* to
their *total outcomes*. The only question that mattered was how much good came out
the other end, not how the sausage was made.

## Funds that adapt to the world

The third conviction is why funds beat one-off donations.

Because the model was built on weighted outputs, it could evolve as a cause
evolved. A climate fund could tilt toward short-term programs to support a region
in the middle of a wildfire. A refugee-relief fund could re-target as the
political reality and the affected populations changed. We were always investing
in the most effective basket of programs, per cause, per region — and the basket
moved when the world did.

## The reporting was the relationship

Holding it all together was the monthly report. It told donors which programs
their money reached, the real-world outcomes it was predicted to generate, and
how our previous forecasts had actually held up. To keep it honest and current,
we ingested a stream of content from every nonprofit in a fund — RSS, email
newsletters, social posts, annual reports — and used LLMs to parse, summarize,
and analyze all of it through the lens of the fund it belonged to. That's how we
told a donor what each organization was working on this month, and how it fit the
larger thesis they'd bought into.

Impact Labs didn't make it. That's a longer story, and one I'm still figuring out
how to tell. But the convictions still hold up, and I'd build on them again.
