Plotpoint was an exercise in using technology to encourage physical connection
with your community. It looked like a phone app — you signed up, you opened a
story, you tapped through levels — but the whole point was to get you *off* the
phone: out the door, into your city, and standing shoulder to shoulder with your
friends in a park, solving something ridiculous together. Think scavenger hunt
meets Pokémon Go meets a murder-mystery dinner party. To progress, you go to a
real place, solve a clue, and unlock the next one.

## It started as a proposal

Plotpoint didn't begin as a company. It began as a way to propose to my wife.

In June of 2024, the ring was burning a hole in my pocket and Malak had set clear
requirements: it should feel specific to us, it should feel like real effort, and
— non-negotiable — no public spectacles. She's someone who already knows about the
new coffee shop in the Richmond and the secret garden down the alley, so a
sentimental stroll through the past wouldn't do. It had to be about the future:
new places, new memories.

So I built a fake company. I wrote a short story about a detective chasing an art
heist and her missing ex-partner, and I turned San Francisco into the game board.
A QR code planted at Ritual Coffee in Hayes Valley. A morse-code transmission to
decode. A scripted night tour through Chinatown. A word hidden in a specific
library edition of Shakespeare, unlocked by unscrambling the act, scene, and line.

It was held together with tape and prayer. The front end was Webflow; the back
end was an extremely janky Cloudflare Worker — the first meaningful code I'd ever
written. Everything was hard-coded, and I had to manually rebuild the app at each
step to unlock the next chapter. Twilio rejected my application to send texts, so
I set up a fake Google Voice number and secretly fed us hints while she wasn't
looking. The morning of, the florist bailed to sleep in, so my friend Noah
cleaned out a Trader Joe's while I pretended to be incapable of finding parking to
buy him time.

It was a mess. It was also a lot of fun, and — most importantly — it worked. And
it left me with a thought I couldn't shake: the *format* was the good part. A
story you physically walk through, with real stakes and real people, is a kind of
magic that doesn't come out of a screen.

## For real this time

A year later I decided to do it again — but as an actual product, and on a
deadline I picked to hurt: launch a real, playable story at
[Outside Lands](https://www.sfoutsidelands.com/), a music festival in Golden Gate
Park, on August 8, 2025. About two and a half weeks out.

The compressed timeline was the most important decision I made, because it forced
every other one. Two seven-day sprints: build the app, then build the story. No
time to second-guess tone, so I let it be exactly as unhinged as I am.

I could have vibe-coded the whole thing, but I didn't. Partly discipline — I'm not
skilled enough to afford spaghetti I can't debug — and partly ego. I think of
myself as a craftsman, which is not always a good thing. I used AI heavily for
syntax and debugging, the places where I knew enough to check its work, and
hand-built the rest. The stack was Supabase (database, auth), Cloudflare
(hosting), and Tanstack Start on the front end, styled with shadcn and Tailwind.

## A box of legos

Here's where the two-weeks discipline broke down, on purpose. If the only goal was
one game at one festival, I'd have hard-coded it. But I wanted a *platform* —
somewhere any writer could set a story in the places that mattered to them.

So underneath the one story is a real content model: **stories** contain
**pages** (the things you actually read — chapters, images, audio), pages are
gated by **challenges**, and **saves** track how far each player has gotten.
Keeping challenges in their own modular tables — the boring wisdom of database
normalization — meant I could invent new kinds of gates without disturbing
anything else. A box of legos: Waypoint and Compass and Secret Location gates for
the map, Password and Passcode gates for the mind, a Timer gate to keep a level
locked until the festival gates actually opened.

## The Touring Test

The launch story was called *The Touring Test.* You play a field debugger hired by
Kempelen Labs, an AI startup whose fully-synthetic popstar, MIRA — she writes her
own songs, runs her own shows, talks to her own fans — is about to headline the
festival, and is very much malfunctioning. Over five levels, you have to fix her.

The name was the whole secret. A few eagle-eyed players caught it: **Kempelen
Labs** is a nod to Wolfgang von Kempelen, the 18th-century engineer behind the
[Mechanical Turk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_Turk) — a chess-playing
"automaton" that dazzled European courts and was, in fact, just a person hiding
inside the machine. That con is the spine of the entire game, and it pays off at
the very end.

## Doing things that don't scale

Around the tenth LinkedIn post, I realized I was hiding online from the one thing
the product actually demanded: going outside and talking to people. So I designed
a flyer in thirty seconds, printed a hundred copies on highlighter-green paper,
and taped roughly eighty of them to poles across Valencia, Hayes, and Divisadero.
I felt shy and self-conscious the entire time, and my hands were disgusting
afterward. It worked better than everything digital combined.

I pointed those flyers at a Partiful event, not my own waitlist — I didn't want a
captive email list, I wanted people who were genuinely excited. And I let the same
philosophy bleed into the product: instead of building reusable templates, I made
a completely bespoke experience for Outside Lands, most of which I'd never reuse.
More work, worse leverage, better story. Paul Graham gave me the permission slip.

## The launch

I'd set modest targets: 100 RSVPs, 50 players, and — mostly — pull it off without
everything exploding.

We got **465 RSVPs** and **130 players** across the weekend. And then I watched, in
real time, as the funnel fell off a cliff: only **33.8% of players made it past
the password puzzle**, and just **5.4%** finished the game. Seven of our eight
support requests that weekend were about that first level. (The eighth was the
servers going down, because I was on a broke-boy Cloudflare plan and had to
upgrade mid-festival.) Someone also stole my NFC tags, which — why?

The lesson was obvious in hindsight: I'd built the difficulty curve backwards. The
hardest, most punishing puzzle was the *first* thing players hit, at a festival,
where hundreds of things — friends, acts, food, drink, bathroom lines — were
competing for their attention. I play video games. I should have known you ramp
up, not open with the boss. A festival turned out to be a genuinely bad place to
launch something that asks for focus.

But the businesses surprised me. I messaged sixteen food vendors two days before
the festival; eight replied, and six said yes to hosting NFC nodes. Nobody could
swing a promo that late, but the appetite was real, and I filed it away for later.

## Was it a success?

Our servers went down. Someone robbed me of my tags. The whole thing was uneven
and often janky, and most people never saw the ending I was proudest of. And yet:
we blew past every target, 130 strangers and friends spent a chunk of their
festival playing a story I wrote, and I learned more in two weeks than in the year
before it.

> "If you're not embarrassed by the first version of your product, you've launched
> too late." — Reid Hoffman

Check, and check. Building in public kept me honest, aggressive timelines keep me
focused, and I still believe the friction is the fun — as long as you're suffering
through it next to someone you like.
